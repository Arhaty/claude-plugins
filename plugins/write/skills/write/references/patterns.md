# Patterns to strip

Tiered by strength. Tier 1 and Tier 2 justify an edit on a single sighting. Tier 3 counts only
when several appear in one passage, because a careful writer uses any one of them on purpose.

The project's guide wins over this list. A guide can relax a Tier 2 or Tier 3 pattern (a casual
voice may allow a fragment; a formal one may require the passive), never a Tier 1.

## Tier 1: fires on sight, any writer

### 1. Not X but Y, and its disguises

"It's not just X, it's Y." "This doesn't mean X. It means Y." "Not only... but also."

The disguises are the ones that get through, because they don't use the word "not":

- "X **rather than** Y"
- "X **instead of** Y"
- "less about X, more about Y"
- "X, not Y" as an appositive

Test: is Y a real alternative someone proposed, or one invented to make X look better? If
invented, delete the whole contrast and keep X.

> Bad: reads the trends out of 400 support tickets rather than asking anyone what they think
>
> Good: the trends come from 400 support tickets

### 2. Arguing with no one

Answering an objection, dismissing an alternative, or naming a lazy version nobody raised.

"This isn't just another benchmark report." "A tempting approach would be to survey people." "Most
people think X. They're wrong." "Contrary to popular belief."

Keep it only if a real person said it. Otherwise state the claim on its own.

### 3. Aphorism and bow closers

"X isn't Y, it's Z." "X is the Y of Z." "The future isn't coming, it's already here." "At its
core, what matters is..."

The closing line an editor deletes most often. End on the last real point instead.

### 4. Faux-insight setups

"What nobody tells you is..." "The part everyone misses..." "Here's what's really going on." "The
dirty secret of X."

Say the thing. If it's insightful, the reader will notice.

### 5. Colon reveals

"The best part: it learns." "The result: a 40% lift." "One problem: nobody uses it."

Occasionally fine. As a habit it's a tell. Use a full sentence.

### 6. Staged run-ups

"Let's dive in." "Here's what you need to know." "Let's break this down." "Honestly? It depends."
"First, some context."

Delete and start at the point.

### 7. Em dashes and en dashes

Period, comma, colon or parentheses. An en dash in a numeric range only where the guide allows it.

### 8. Directness-signalling openers

"Honestly", "to be honest", "let me be honest", "to be frank", "look,", "here's the thing", "the
thing is". A statement that needs a preamble announcing its honesty is too weak to stand alone.

## Tier 2: fires on sight in the default voice

### 9. Forced triads

"Faster, smarter, cleaner." Three examples plus a lesson. Three-clause parallel lists for rhythm.
Use the number of items the meaning needs, usually one or two.

### 10. Inflated significance

"Stands as a testament", "marks a pivotal moment", "underscores the importance", "plays a key
role", "in an ever-evolving landscape", "highlighting the team's commitment to". Keep the fact,
drop the significance.

### 11. Sales language

"Nestled within", "breathtaking", "seamless", "game-changing", "unlock", "elevate",
"supercharge". State what the thing is.

### 12. Weasel attribution

"Experts agree", "studies show", "research suggests", "it's widely known". Name the source and
what it said, or cut the claim.

### 13. Chatbot residue

"I hope this helps", "Let me know if", "Would you like me to", "Feel free to", "Certainly!",
"Great question."

### 14. Sycophancy

"Great question", "You're absolutely right", "Excellent point", "That's a really smart way to
think about it."

### 15. Bold-label bullets

"**Performance:** Performance improved." The mechanical "**Label:** sentence" on every bullet.
Bold for real emphasis inside prose is fine, and so is a label that names a term being defined.

### 16. Closing pleasantries

"Happy to chat", "Feel free to reach out", "Don't hesitate", "Looking forward to hearing from
you". End on the substance or a direct question.

### 17. Copula avoidance

"Serves as", "stands as", "acts as", "boasts", "features" where "is" or "has" works. Use "is".

### 18. Signposting and self-narration

Any sentence describing what the piece is doing: "This page measures...", "In this section we'll
look at...", "The table below shows...". Let the content do it.

Same family: narrating the method ("compiled from public sources", "anything unconfirmed is
flagged rather than guessed") and what the text replaced ("this was added to replace the old
approach"). Keep a source credit the reader can follow and a caveat that changes what they'd do.
Cut the account of how the work was made. A previous version belongs only in changelogs and
release notes.

In product copy, internal scoring, thresholds and process steps are method narration. Say what the
reader gets.

### 19. Explaining the obvious

A sentence telling the reader what a number means when the number already said it. "That's a big
gap." "This is significant."

Includes vague declaratives that announce weight without naming the thing ("The implications are
significant", "The stakes are high", "The reasons are structural") and lines that tell the reader
how to read ("That last part matters more than it sounds", "The key point is", "This distinction
matters", "Let that sink in", "Full stop."). Name the specific implication or cut the line.

### 20. Dramatic fragments

Verbless sentences used for punch. "Four weeks." "No prior. No nostalgia." "That's it."

Fires on a single sighting when it opens a paragraph or section, or stands alone as a sentence.
Fold the fact into a real sentence, or cut it if the information appears elsewhere (a table, a
heading).

Not a tell: greetings, sign-offs ("Thanks"), headings, bullets, table cells, or a one-word answer
to a direct question.

### 21. False agency

Inanimate things doing human verbs so nobody has to be named. "The data tells us", "the market
rewards", "the decision emerged", "the complaint became a fix", "the culture shifted". Name who
did it: "we read the data", "buyers paid for", "the team fixed it".

### 22. Vague role words

"Involved in", "contributed to", "associated with", "supported", "played a part in", "worked on"
where a specific role exists. They either undersell (they led it) or oversell (they were adjacent
to it). Name the role the facts support: led, owned, built, ran, reported to.

### 23. Announcement openers

"Excited to announce", "Thrilled to share", "Proud to present", "I'm happy to say". State what
happened: "We shipped X." "I joined Y as head of design."

### 24. Audience sweeps and empty merisms

"Whether you're a startup founder or an enterprise CTO...", "from startups to enterprises", "from
concept to launch", "for beginners and pros alike". Pick the actual reader and talk to them, or
name what's really covered.

### 25. Paired adjectives

"Simple yet powerful", "fast and reliable", "lightweight but comprehensive". Pick the stronger
one, then back it with a fact ("loads in 200 ms").

### 26. Mechanical transitions

Sentence-initial "Additionally", "Moreover", "Furthermore", "That said", "With that in mind", "In
conclusion". Use "and", "but", "so", or nothing. The next thought is usually the transition.

### 27. Rhetorical-question setups

"What if you could...?", "So what does this mean? It means...", "Why does this matter?" used to
open a section or stage the next sentence. State the answer. Keep a question only when the reader
would ask it and the answer adds a fact. One real question at the end of a message ("Worth a short
call?") is an ask, and fine.

### 41. Feature-list sentence

One sentence chains three or more capability verbs, often verb-first with no subject. It is the
default shape of AI product copy, and every product could use it.

> Bad: Drafts and edits text in your project's voice, then quotes each line that reads as
> AI-written and names the pattern.
>
> Bad: Plan each change in a spec, build it behind a pull request, and update docs/canon/ in the
> same pull request.
>
> Bad: It drafts in your project's voice, strips AI writing tells, and checks a draft against 40
> named patterns.
>
> Bad: Write a spec, build it behind a pull request, keep docs/canon/ current, and let hooks guard
> your branches.
>
> Bad: canon writes one spec per change, carries it through review, staging, and a release pull
> request, keeps docs/canon/ current as the project's living documentation, and enforces the git
> rules with hooks.
>
> Good: write checks a draft for AI writing tells and quotes each line that has one, with the
> pattern it breaks. New drafts follow your project's style guide.
>
> Good: Each change starts as a spec and ships in a pull request that also updates docs/canon/.
> Hooks refuse pushes and merges into your protected branches.

In product, site and README copy, one or two verbs per sentence. Three or more capabilities go in
a list or a table, never one sentence.

Not a tell: a plain list of nouns ("emails, posts, docs, READMEs, and replies"), or a literal step
in a numbered procedure ("Cuts a branch, researches, and writes docs/specs/NNN-slug.md").

### 42. Reassurance rider

A clause tacked on to soothe the reader that adds no fact: "before you see it", "before it reaches
you", "before it hands anything back", "so you don't have to", "without lifting a finger", "in
seconds", "with no extra work". The phrase-level form of manufactured warmth.

> Bad: Claude writes a first draft, then rereads it for AI patterns and fixes them before you see
> it.
>
> Good: Claude writes a first draft, then rereads it for AI patterns and fixes them.

Cut the rider. If the timing is a real fact the reader needs, state it as one.

## Tier 3: weak alone, count when stacked

### 28. Stacked qualifiers and smeared hedging

"Could potentially possibly be argued." Keep only the qualifiers the evidence supports: one hedge
per real limitation, placed on that claim. The opposite also counts: a long piece where no
sentence is unsure of anything.

### 29. Hyphenated pairs everywhere

Keep only the hyphens grammar needs.

### 30. Passive voice and missing subjects

"No configuration file needed." Name the actor when it helps. In methods sections the passive is
often correct, so don't over-apply.

### 31. Repeated sentence openings

"She noted... She noted... She filed..." Merge or change the subject.

### 32. Synonym cycling

Calling the same thing three names to avoid repetition. "The agent handles email. The assistant
drafts replies. The system files them." Repeat the noun.

### 33. Shallow -ing riders

"..., ensuring...", "..., allowing you to...", "..., reflecting...", "..., showcasing..." tacked
on for depth. Cut, or make it a real clause.

### 34. Curly quotes

Straight quotes, unless the guide sets typographic quotes.

### 35. Heading echoed in the first sentence

"## Performance" followed by "Performance matters." Let the heading do the work.

### 36. Knowledge-limit disclaimers

"While details are limited...", "it appears that...", "based on available information". State
what is known or cut the sentence.

### 37. Lazy extremes

"Every", "always", "never", "everyone", "nobody" doing vague work ("Every marketer knows...").
Use the real scope or a number. Fine when literally true.

### 38. A punchline closing every paragraph

Each paragraph lands on a short aphoristic line. One per piece is a choice. Three is a template.
Demote the rest to plain statements or cut them. This is the tell that survives a cleanup pass,
because each line looks clean on its own.

### 39. The same point twice

The thesis restated in each section in new words ("Each one is forgivable. Stacked, they're a
fingerprint." then later "Alone they mean nothing, together they add up."). Say it once, where
it's strongest, and use the space for a point that moves the argument.

### 40. Exclamation marks

More than one in a piece.

### 43. Repeated phrase

The same distinctive phrase recurring across one piece, often once per section. One page used
"before you see it" four times and "Claude never merges into production" three times. #39 is the
same point twice; this is the same words twice.

A phrase of four or more words, other than the product's name and its commands, appears at most
once per piece. Say it where it is strongest, and use different words or nothing elsewhere.

## Vocabulary: cut or replace

delve, leverage (verb), tapestry, landscape (abstract), vibrant, crucial, pivotal, robust, foster,
garner, showcase, underscore, testament, seamless, elevate, unlock, navigate (figurative), realm,
intricate, myriad, boasts, nestled, groundbreaking, game-changer, deep dive, at scale (as filler),
best-in-class, cutting-edge, holistic, synergy, empower, streamline, harness, embark, journey
(figurative), resonate, curate, bespoke (unless literal), meticulous, transformative, additionally
(sentence-initial), moreover, furthermore, bolster, interplay, utilize, facilitate, paradigm,
ecosystem (figurative), multifaceted, paramount, spearhead, encompass, beacon, ever-evolving,
quietly (as color: "quietly reshaping").

Phrases: at the heart of, in today's [world/landscape], in an era where, the intersection of,
paving the way, it remains to be seen, move the needle, double down, lean into, circle back,
low-hanging fruit, take it to the next level, unlock the full potential, the real question is, at
its core, in your voice, in your brand voice, in your project's voice, in your own words, sounds
like you, sounds just like you (name where the voice comes from: "writes to your project's style
guide"), before you see it, before it reaches you, before it hands anything back, so you don't
have to, without lifting a finger, in seconds, with no extra work (riders, #42).

Filler swaps: "in order to" is "to". "Due to the fact that" is "because". "It's important to note
that" goes. "A number of" is the number.

## Format tells

No emojis. Straight quotes. Sentence case headings, not Title Case. No decorative arrows or
symbols in headings. No bold as decoration. No "Key takeaways", "Conclusion" or "Challenges and
outlook" sections, and no "Despite these challenges, X continues to..." ending. A heading names
what the section holds, not a tagline ("How the six options compare", not "The decision, on one
screen"). No hashtag blocks.

Chatbot debris, delete on sight: `utm_source=chatgpt.com` or `utm_source=openai` in links,
`[cite: N]`, `oaicite`, `turn0search0`, placeholders like `[Your Name]` or `2025-XX-XX`, `---`
before every heading, code fences around prose.
